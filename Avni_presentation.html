01 · Introduction

A lie dressed up as a debate

Have you ever wondered how powerful industries manage to keep selling harmful products while real experts scream for them to be stopped? Their secret weapon is disinformation; a carefully crafted and distributed outright lie designed to mislead the public and protect corporate profits. When science threatens a profitable business model, corporations simply launch campaigns to cloud public understanding and delay regulations. One of the main methods used in this deception is science denial: the creation of false controversies to disguise settled scientific facts.

This is the same tactic that public relations practitioner John Hill employed decades ago to put the mask of fake experts on the deadly connection between tobacco and cancer. Today, that same playbook is used on social media by groups such as PragerU to mask the climate crisis.

The only way to look through the bluster of these manipulators and see the truth is to understand how they transform falsehoods to convincing arguments.
02 · How it works

The Mechanics of Science Denial

Science denial is a disinformation strategy that corporations use when they want to make proven facts look like an ongoing debate (Rigged, 2021a). Rather than simply lying, companies will pay fringe researchers or put together fake grassroots groups to bring up the small uncertainties. This tricks the general public into thinking that we need to do more research before taking action.

🧑‍🔬

Fringe experts

Paid researchers who lend credibility to a minority view.

🌱

Fake grassroots

Front groups that look independent but serve the industry.

⏳

"More research"

Small uncertainties stretched into a reason to delay action.

A good historical example of this would be the tobacco industry in the 1950s. Back then, early medical studies were finally connecting smoking to lung cancer, which obviously threatened tobacco sales. To protect the industry, an advertising executive named John Hill launched a massive science denial campaign (Rigged, 2021c). He helped tobacco companies form the Tobacco Industry Research Committee and run a nationwide newspaper ad called the "Frank Statement to Cigarette Smokers". The ad promised to fund independent research while at the same time spreading alternative, unproven theories about what causes cancer.

The tobacco industry successfully made the health risks of smoking look like an open question by giving a platform to a few opposing doctors (Rigged, 2021c). Even though smoking causes cancer, this fake uncertainty lasted for decades and was constantly used to delay government health regulations. This campaign is an early example of creating a fake controversy that convinces people that corporations are just trying to find the objective truth.

03 · Why it works

The Psychology Behind the Doubt

On a psychological level, science denial works so well because of something called "motivated reasoning" (Shane, 2020). As humans, we feel deeply uncomfortable when new information goes against our existing desires or behaviors. Consumers usually know what they enjoy doing, and it is tough for them to accept scientific facts that say their everyday habits are harmful. Taking that science seriously would mean they have to make difficult lifestyle changes.

🧠

Motivated reasoning

Seeking out information we already want to believe, even against overwhelming evidence.

🔍

Confirmation bias

Noticing and remembering details that confirm what we already think, while ignoring the rest.

Because of this discomfort, the public often seeks out information that they already want to believe, even if there is overwhelming scientific evidence proving the opposite (Shane, 2020). People eagerly accept any bit of doubt offered by corporate-funded campaigns. When front groups or opposing, scientists claim that science is still unsettled, consumers use that doubt as a psychological escape. It gives them an easy excuse to dismiss the uncomfortable truth, and they view the issue as a genuine debate rather than a calculated corporate distraction. A similar concept is confirmation bias, which is when consumers focus on and remember details that confirm their pre-existing beliefs while ignoring objective facts (Shane, 2020).

04 · The architect

A Historical Blueprint: John Hill

Capitalizing on the psychological vulnerabilities of the public required a mastermind, and the foundational rules for science denial were ultimately created by a man named John Hill (Rigged, 2021c). He was born on a farm in Indiana in 1890. His father lost all of the family's money in bad business deals, so Hill spent his whole life trying to win the approval of rich men to get his status back. Working as a business reporter for 17 years gave him valuable experience before he eventually started his own public relations company.

StylePlain conservative suit, wire-rimmed glasses, hair neatly slicked back.
HabitHated the spotlight and almost never spoke to the press.

Unlike flashy advertising executives who craved the spotlight, Hill hated being the center of attention and almost never spoke to the press. It became his trademark to be behind the scenes and quiet. Whenever people actually caught a glimpse of him, the quiet strategist was always dressed in a plain conservative suit and wire-rimmed glasses, with his hair neatly slicked back (Rigged, 2021c). Despite such an unassuming appearance, the secretive executive had a massive historical impact by inventing the playbook for corporate disinformation.

The bosses of all the major American tobacco companies secretly met with Hill at the Plaza Hotel in December 1953 (Rigged, 2021b). They needed his help because medical studies were showing that smoking was dangerous. At this meeting, they launched the Tobacco Industry Research Committee. Hill helped them hide the dangers of smoking by paying fake experts. For example, he hired a man named Dr. Clarence Cook Little. To make the public question the doctors, Hill made a big noise about this man being a top cancer expert (Rigged, 2021c). Hill kept the tobacco companies looking innocent by promoting his own hand picked scientists. Meanwhile, regular people thought they were watching a real scientific debate. Because of this strategy, Hill built the biggest public relations firm in the world before he died in 1977.

Companies today are still using these same tactics. Now, oil and gas companies run social media ads pretending that climate change is not a proven fact. Such campaign is a modern example of science denial. Just like John Hill did with cigarettes, oil companies try to trick everyday people. They want the public to believe there's still debate on the environment among experts. Companies like this pay for articles and pictures that make science look confusing. They use science denial so they can keep polluting without anyone trying to stop them (Rigged, 2021a).

05 · Today

Contemporary Science Denial on Social Media

Just like John Hill used fake experts in the past, modern companies use the exact same tricks on social media today. Wealthy oil and gas groups often spend millions of dollars on online ads that pretend climate change is not a real problem so they can protect their profits (Rigged, 2021a).

For example, a group called PragerU promoted a Facebook video titled "Climate Change: What Do Scientists Say?" featuring Richard Lindzen. While the individual post shows 1.6K likes, the broader ad campaign behind it in which one scientist chosen carefully told over 14.2 million viewers that global warming is entirely harmless (PragerU, 2016). It was backed by an estimated $6.9 million in advertising spend (Avaaz, 2021). Putting a single questioning scientist in a professional looking video perfectly copies John Hill's old strategy which is tricking everyday people into thinking that most experts are still arguing about the facts. Ultimately, creating this fake confusion online gives the public an easy excuse to ignore the climate crisis, which lets big polluters keep making money without the government stepping in.

The modern PragerU campaign is effectively the same approach as that used by John Hill for the tobacco industry. Like Hill did in the 1930s with the Tobacco Industry Research Committee to support fringe doctors such as Dr. Clarence Cook Little who has dismissed the connection between smoking and lung cancer (Rigged, 2021c), PragerU boosts the dissenters, such as Lindzen. The same playbook is used in both campaigns. If one portrays a minority opinion in an authoritative manner, it is able to successfully create doubt in the public eye. Everyday consumers cannot easily establish complex scientific claims, so including a claimed expert is sufficient to keep the government away from regulation and keep corporate needs in front of the consumer.

The science denial technique continues to be a massive hit online for a few reasons, including human psychological vulnerabilities. As discussed earlier, people engage in motivated reasoning and confirmation bias, naturally seeking out information that supports their pre-existing beliefs while avoiding uncomfortable truths (Shane, 2020). If social media algorithms deliver a professional presentation of a credentialed scientist to a user who already doesn't trust government regulation or the mainstream media, the user will readily accept it. The fake expert gives audiences a comfortable psychological excuse to ignore the emerging climate crisis. Ultimately, exploiting mental shortcuts accelerates the spread of disinformation and allows major polluters to continue operating without facing immediate consequences.

06 · Conclusion

Seeing past the illusion

Manufacturing doubt continues to be one of the most dangerous and effective weapons in the corporate public relations arsenal. Decades after John Hill first realized that promoting fringe experts could shield the tobacco industry from accountability, modern organizations heavily rely on identical tactics to confuse the public about climate change. Campaigns like PragerU are able to scale disinformation at exponential rates on digital platforms, a perfect fit for human confirmation bias and motivated reasoning.

Recognizing the mechanics of science denial is absolutely essential for navigating the modern information landscape. Until everyday consumers learn to look past the illusion of academic debate, wealthy polluters will continue to delay vital regulations while maximizing their financial returns at the expense of public health.
